# 2038

## Chapter One

Winston Reed finished work at five o’clock.

He had spent eight hours selecting football catalogs and was rather pleased with himself. His score had been good, and several of his selections had turned out exactly as he had hoped. A good score meant more than a pleasant number at the end of the day. It made a difference to what he could have afterward, and Reed had a few things in mind for the evening.

He looked through his work log once more before leaving. There were the football situations he had preferred, the changes he had suggested, and the choices he had made throughout the afternoon. He liked looking back over them. It gave him a sense of what he had accomplished.

The catalogs were generally called Cats. Reed couldn't remember the last time he'd heard anyone use the full word in conversation.

He had always liked football, and he now spent most of his working life deciding which football he liked best. There were worse ways to spend a working day.

Outside, Brackton was busy with people finishing their own workdays. Reed headed toward the pub, where he expected to find Charleston and Benjamin.

He remembered when nobody had believed jobs like his would exist.

It had been 2027, when the transistors were beginning to do things that people had spent their entire lives learning how to do. There had been a great deal of worry about employment. Reed had worried himself.

Then people began receiving offers to spend their days choosing between catalogs.

Reed had been offered football.

He remembered looking at the offer and wondering who on earth would reward him for something he'd been doing voluntarily for years.

He'd thought it was a scam.

His roommate at the time had received an offer to select anime catalogs.

“Look at this,” his roommate had said. “They're offering me a job picking anime characters.”

“That's not a job,” Reed had replied.

“It says it is.”

“Well, mine says I can work picking football.”

They had both been suspicious.

Then the first scores came through.

And people discovered the scores were worth something. They could get the things they needed, and good work gave them more choice. Reed had waited for someone to explain the catch. Nobody ever had.

His roommate eventually moved out and spent his working days choosing anime characters, scenes, and the more unusual material that interested him. He enjoyed the work enormously. Reed didn't see him as often anymore, though he occasionally heard about how well things were going.

Reed had never shared his enthusiasm for anime, but he understood it well enough. His former roommate spent eight hours doing something he loved, and his selections gave him access to the things he wanted.

So was Reed.

Music had gone much the same way.

One of his friends had been terribly worried about what the transistors would do to music. He'd thought there would be no reason for anyone to bother with it anymore.

Instead, he found himself selecting music catalogs, comparing songs and choosing which versions he preferred.

The transistors could produce the music, but people still had opinions about what they wanted to hear. One person preferred one version, another preferred something else, and those preferences kept changing.

Reed didn't know exactly what happened to all those selections afterward. He knew that the music he heard seemed to suit him better than it once had, and that his friend was delighted to be contributing to it.

That was enough for him.

The same thing had happened across nearly every sort of work. People who once spent their days producing things now spent their days selecting what they preferred. They still had jobs, sometimes with the very same titles as before, though much of the work had become a matter of choosing Cats. Their contributions were scored, and the scores mattered when they wanted something. Generally, people seemed much happier with the arrangement.

Reed certainly was.

He reached the pub and went inside.

Charleston and Benjamin were already sitting together, watching the television.

“Evening, lads,” Reed said.

“Evening, Reed,” Charleston replied. “How was work?”

“Very good, actually.”

“Good score?”

“Very good score.”

Benjamin smiled. “You say that every day.”

“Well, I've been doing very good work.”

Reed sat down beside them. He had barely settled before his drink was ready. His day’s contribution was already accounted for; there was no bill to settle and nothing to hand over. He took the glass with the quiet satisfaction of a man whose work had gone well.

“What have you two been doing?”

“Music,” Benjamin said.

“Still?”

Benjamin looked at him.

“What else would I be doing?”

“Fair point.”

Charleston laughed.

Reed glanced toward the television. A football game was playing, and he recognised a clip almost immediately.

“Hang on a moment,” he said.

Neither of his friends looked away from the screen.

“I picked that.”

Charleston turned toward him.

“Picked what?”

“That clip. I had that one at work today.”

“You did?”

“Yes. There were two versions. I picked that one.”

Reed watched it with considerable satisfaction.

It was strange, in a pleasant sort of way, seeing something he'd selected earlier in the day appear on the television that evening.

He hadn't made the entire football game. He knew that perfectly well. But he had helped decide how part of it ought to be, and now there it was.

“I thought that was the better one,” he said.

Benjamin nodded.

“Looks good.”

“Exactly.”

Charleston looked at Reed.

“You're very pleased with yourself.”

“I should be. I spent eight hours working on this sort of thing.”

“Eight hours watching football?”

“Eight hours selecting football. There's a difference.”

“Of course there is.”

Reed smiled and turned back to the television.

A commercial appeared.

Benjamin suddenly leaned forward.

“Oh, listen to this.”

The commercial began playing a song.

Reed listened.

“That's mine,” Benjamin said.

“What is?”

“The music. I picked that version.”

Charleston looked at him.

“You picked that song?”

“Not the whole song. I picked that version over another one. Listen.”

They listened.

Benjamin looked delighted.

“That's the one I preferred. The other version wasn't quite as good.”

Reed nodded.

“Well, you were right.”

“Thank you.”

“I like that one.”

“So do I. That's why I picked it.”

Charleston laughed.

The three of them watched the rest of the commercial.

Reed thought it was rather nice. He'd just seen his own work on the television, and now Benjamin had seen his. Neither of them had expected to be doing anything like this ten years earlier.

Charleston had his own work in advertising. He spent his days choosing between arrangements of pictures, lettering, and other details, helping decide what looked best.

Before the transistors, advertising had been a different sort of profession. Charleston was still an advertiser, as far as anyone was concerned. Now he spent his day concentrating on the Cats he enjoyed.

He seemed perfectly happy with it.

“Funny, isn't it?” Reed said.

“What is?” Charleston asked.

“All of this. Remember when we thought nobody would have a job?”

Benjamin laughed.

“I remember.”

“We thought those offers were scams.”

“Some of us did,” Charleston said.

“I certainly did,” Reed replied. “Someone tells you that choosing football all day will get you everything you need. What are you supposed to think?”

“That you're very lucky,” Benjamin said.

“Well, I know that now.”

Reed looked around the pub.

It was a cheerful place that evening. People were talking about their days, discussing what they'd selected and what they'd seen. Everyone seemed to have something they were interested in.

There were still disagreements, of course. Reed had heard plenty of arguments over which Cat was better. He'd been involved in several himself.

But he rather liked that.

He had never expected everyone to agree about football, music, or anything else.

Across the room, he noticed the doctor.

The man was sitting alone.

Reed recognised him from years earlier, before the transistors had taken over most medical work. He had once been a very important doctor, the sort of man people waited a long time to see.

He looked miserable.

“There's the doctor,” Reed said.

Charleston glanced over.

“Oh, yes.”

“Doesn't look very happy.”

“He never does,” Benjamin replied.

The doctor was sitting with a drink in front of him, staring at nothing in particular.

Reed remembered what medical care used to be like.

Years earlier, he'd needed heart surgery. Back then the operation had cost him an extraordinary amount of money. He'd spent ages paying for it.

Only the previous week he'd gone for a medical checkup. A transistor had examined him, and that was that. No fee, no bill, no worry about whether he could afford to go.

The difference still amazed him.

He looked at the doctor again.

“I went for a checkup the other week,” Reed said.

“How did it go?” Charleston asked.

“Perfectly well. Didn't have to pay for a thing.”

“Good.”

“Remember when we had to pay for it?”

Benjamin nodded.

“Terrible.”

“I had that heart operation years ago. Cost me an arm and a leg back then.”

Charleston looked amused.

“Not literally, I hope.”

“No, thankfully. I'd have had to pay extra for those.”

Benjamin laughed.

Reed took another look at the doctor.

The man still had a comfortable life. He still worked in medicine, although much of his work now involved selecting medical Cats. The transistors handled things that had once required his particular expertise.

Reed understood that the doctor didn't like the change.

He had once been someone people treated with tremendous importance. Now most people could get excellent medical care without needing to know who he was.

That seemed to bother him terribly.

Reed found it difficult to sympathise.

“I don't know what he's so upset about,” he said. “It's much better now.”

“Perhaps he preferred the old work,” Benjamin suggested.

“Maybe he did. But I certainly preferred not paying him all that money.”

Charleston smiled.

“That's because you were the one paying back then.”

“Exactly.”

Reed looked at the doctor for another moment.

He didn't wish the man any harm. He simply couldn't understand wanting things to go back to the way they had been.

The doctor had been important, certainly. But Reed had needed his heart fixed, not an important man.

And the transistor had managed his latest examination perfectly well.

“Anyway,” Reed said, turning back to his friends, “what's wrong with selecting medical Cats? Everyone else is selecting something.”

Benjamin nodded.

“I suppose he misses doing the operations.”

“Perhaps. But I don't miss paying for them.”

That made Charleston laugh.

The football returned to the television.

Reed recognised another part of the game and watched with interest. Benjamin was still pleased about the song in the commercial, and Charleston had begun talking about some advertising selections he'd made earlier in the week.

They compared their work for a while, occasionally disagreeing and occasionally congratulating one another.

Reed was enjoying himself.

He'd had a good day at work, earned a good score, and seen something he'd selected appear on the television. His friends had done much the same with their own interests.

Ten years earlier, he would never have believed that this was what working life would become.

He'd been quite frightened of the future then.

Now, sitting with Charleston and Benjamin, he couldn't imagine wanting the old days back.

“Another good day, then?” Benjamin asked.

“Very good,” Reed replied.

“Looking forward to tomorrow?”

“Of course.”

“More football?”

“Eight hours of it.”

Charleston shook his head, smiling.

“And a good score gets you a good evening.”

“It certainly does.”

Reed looked at the television once more.

“Quite a good arrangement, really.”
