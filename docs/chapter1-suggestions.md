## 2. Too much exposition before the pub

**Where:** ll.17–63, plus the repeated scam memory at ll.201–215

**Bible basis:**
- "Let the world appear through everyday interactions, transactions, banter and social manners, not lengthy exposition or lectures."
- "Never turn the novel into a technical explainer."

**Suggestion:** We're about 60 lines in before Reed reaches the pub. The scam/roommate memory is told in narration and then retold as banter, so keep only the banter version. Cut the economic summary at l.63 ("The same thing had happened across nearly every sort of work…"). Cut the unnamed "one of his friends" music section (ll.51–61) too, because Benjamin shows the same thing on screen at the pub. If you want to keep the roommate, put him in a single line of dialogue.

## 3. Reed's voice is too genteel

**Where:** throughout, e.g. l.75 "Very good, actually.", l.359 "Quite a good arrangement, really.", l.7 "rather pleased with himself"

**Bible basis:**
- "[Reed's] casual, funny voice is the principal storytelling engine. Preserve spontaneous working-class humor rather than turning him into a mouthpiece for economic theory."
- His sample line: "Why would I bloody want to do that? … now the bloody transistor does it for free."

**Suggestion:** He's a bloke from an industrial city who loves football and the pub, but at the moment he talks like a polite clerk. Rough up the dialogue: let him swear, exaggerate and rib the other two. The narration can stay plain; the dialogue is what needs the change.

*Example:* "Good score?" / "Good? I was robbed of a perfect one by a throw-in."

## 4. Make the "I picked that" beat a rule of three

**Where:** ll.101–195

**Bible basis:**
- "Charleston works in advertising by selecting visual Cats (arrangements of images, lettering and other details)"
- "Pub friends / lads: Football, music and other Cat selectors, bantering about outputs they proudly 'made.'"

**Suggestion:** Reed claims the clip, then Benjamin claims the song in almost the same way. Charleston does visual Cats for advertising and is watching an advert, so he's the obvious third. Have him claim the lettering or layout, and let the three of them argue over whose advert it is. That gives the scene a comic peak.

*Example:*
"Never mind the song. Look at the lettering."
"What about it?"
"Mine. It was up against a green one. Green was horrible."
"So it's my advert, then," Benjamin said.
"It's our advert."
"Has it got football in it?" Reed asked.
"No."
"Then I'm out."

## 6. Paying for heart surgery in England

**Where:** l.249, l.271

**Bible basis:**
- "a fictional English industrial city"
- "I had to pay an arm and a leg for a hospital checkup" (canon line)
- "Characters can recall paying money in 2027 or earlier"

**Suggestion:** A British reader will ask why Reed paid at all, given the NHS. Explain it instead of changing it: the waiting list was enormous, so he went private. That keeps the canon line, makes it more convincingly British, and adds a second joke about the old world.

*Example:* "Eighteen-month waiting list. Eighteen months, with my heart. So I went private. Cost me an arm and a leg."

## 7. Pay off "a few things in mind for the evening"

**Where:** l.7 (setup); ending ll.339–359 (no payoff)

**Bible basis:**
- "Grace: [Reed's] attractive, attentive Mag partner, tuned to his preferences."
- "[Reed's] favorite meat: A specific desired item is temporarily out of reach based on his rewards; he contributes better Cat judgments and happily gets it."

**Suggestion:** Line 7 promises that a good score buys something tonight, but the chapter ends without showing it. Options:
- (a) end with him heading home to Grace;
- (b) a small mention that tonight's score didn't quite stretch to the cut he wanted, which sets up the meat thread for later chapters;
- (c) cut the promise at l.7.

Keep any of these light. The meat disappointment is "petty and comic, not evidence of generalized deprivation."

## 8. (Optional) A glimpse of a Brute on the walk to the pub

**Where:** around l.15 ("Outside, Brackton was busy…")

**Bible basis:**
- "A profoundly absurd, funny, sometimes horrific British social satire"
- "Brutes in public: Towering, unmistakably human protectors, casually insulted by happy citizens; their humiliation is treated as ordinary."
- "The comic and the horrific coexist without forcing characters to comment on that contrast."
- "They are not police of ordinary Cat selectors and never intimidate [Reed] over his work."

**Suggestion:** At the moment the chapter is cosy from start to finish. A few sentences of a Brute doing heavy labour, mocked in passing and walked past without comment, would put the shadow in Chapter One with no lecture. Reed doesn't reflect on it. Leave it out if you'd rather introduce Brutes later.

*Example:* "By the tram stop a Brute was lifting a fallen lamp post back into place while two lads on the bench gave him directions. 'Mind you don't strain your brain, big man,' one said. Reed stepped round them and carried on toward the pub."
