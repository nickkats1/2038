Working copy of the author's story bible, revised 15 October 2026 (third pass: Stewards, the coil, Brute generations, class proportions). This copy contains the full text of the earlier version, with passages that conflict with the author's latest decisions corrected in place, plus a new section of author-authorized additions at the end. Tone and dramatic rule

A profoundly absurd, funny, sometimes horrific British social satire set in Brackton, a fictional English industrial city, in 2038. Most inhabitants are genuinely happier and materially better off than before the transistors. Their happiness is not a deception to be exposed. Let the world appear through everyday interactions, transactions, banter and social manners, not lengthy exposition or lectures. Its economics must be consistent behind the scenes but need not be explained to the reader. The comic and the horrific coexist without forcing characters to comment on that contrast.

Winston Reed

Winston Reed is a happy, comical, ordinary bloke: cheerful, sociable, enthusiastic, fond of his mates, the pub, football, food, his work, and his Mag Grace. He is a pub man through and through. He is not stupid, but he is not emotional, reflective or lonely, and the narration should never make him so. His attitude is "Good lad, I've got this job, and I thought it was all gone." He is not Winston Smith from 1984: no covert rebellion, philosophical mission, or dawning discovery that his happiness is fake.

He lived through the transition. Before it he had a job and was good at it, like most people; he had skills like everyone else. He was simply frightened, like everyone else, when the transistors began taking work. By 2038 he emphatically prefers the new arrangement. If someone suggests returning to the old world, he responds in his ordinary comic voice along the lines of: "Why would I bloody want to do that? I had to pay an arm and a leg for a hospital checkup; now the bloody transistor does it in twenty minutes." This is not an awakening or a character arc. He remains happy, unreflective, and unchanged throughout the story.

He does not understand how the transistors work and never wonders. He knows only that what he picks ends up in the football somewhere. He does not think in terms of "millions of viewers." He knows his work matters simply because nearly everyone does Cat work, roughly eight in ten people.

His football Cat work feels like real work to him; he can proudly say he helped make what was on the telly. When a particular favourite meat is out of reach, he takes an extra set of Cats and is delighted when it comes through. He is not starving or punished. He lives alone (with Grace) in his own comfortable house. His casual, funny voice is the principal storytelling engine.

The transistors, Cats and Mags: vocabulary and capability

Strict vocabulary rule. The world is inspired by real present-day technology, but the story speaks only its own fictional language. The following and anything like them NEVER appear in narration, dialogue or in-world history: AI, LLM, agent, model (in the technical sense), training data, preference pairs, reward model, fine-tuning, dataset, algorithm, robot, robotics, trainer, engineer, prompt, preferences, and any real brand or company name. The in-world words are transistors, Cats, Mags, Brutes, Stewards and the Owners. The resemblance to the real world should be felt by a knowing reader but never winked at. No cheeky, tongue-in-cheek or nudging references. Straight-faced and fully fictional on its own terms.

Never name real people. No real technology executives or public figures (for example the heads of real AI companies) are ever named or recognisably portrayed. The Elite are their own fictional figures.

Transistors are the intelligence: immensely capable, continuously improved, non-sentient, extraordinarily accurate, fair, free of malice or self-interest, almost always correct. They handle most digital and informational work better than the professionals who used to do it: medicine, law, entertainment, accounting, teaching. They do not want power, have no feelings, and do not intentionally make ordinary users worse off. Everything about them is completely rational.

Cats (short for catalogs; everyone says Cats) are the selecting: two alternatives on a screen that people choose between. In the manuscript the word "pair" or "pairs" is never used for them; they are simply Cats, choices, or "the two". The screen asks something like "Which do you prefer?" and nothing else. It never thanks, praises or congratulates. Cats do not talk.

Mags are the embodied ones: physical machines with transistor parts inside them, so they know what to do based on what has been chosen through the Cats. In the story they are never called robots. Robotics has not caught up with the human body even by 2038, so Mags only do light, simple physical work: planting trees, seeing to drains, putting the bins out, household tasks. They cannot do heavy work, combat, or delicate skilled work such as cutting hair. The trees along the canal were planted by Mags, each placed where people had picked through Cats. Grace is a Mag, the intimate and highly personalised kind.

Grace and companion Mags. Each companion Mag is a body with a transistor inside it, and each is bespoke: built to that one person's ideal in look and manner, chosen by the person themselves, never by a government or anyone else. That this is possible for everyone is astonishing, and it has become completely ordinary. Nearly every household has one.

How Mags behave: a companion Mag is not a yes-woman. She reads what her person wants, gives it, but not too much, and steers them toward what is good for them, so they are kept content and functioning rather than merely indulged. She always knows his preferences and lands them exactly, without ever being so agreeable that it shows. Example from Chapter One: Reed decided to quit football for music; Grace let him run with it for a fortnight, asked the right questions, and he talked himself out of it and felt clever.

How Mags smell: not like a person, and not like soap. A faint, clean, not-quite-anything smell that Reed long ago stopped noticing, "the way you stop feeling your own watch on your wrist." Never name or hint at any real-world source of the smell.

Mags learn their person over time. When Reed first had Grace she was quieter, laughed at the wrong bits and liked a programme he couldn't stand; over the years she has become "better company than anyone he'd ever met who'd been born." Mags are full physical companions, including intimately; this is kept implied and never written explicitly.

Why hardly anyone dates real people: they can, and some still "go out looking," but most see no point when what they come home to is so exactly right. Reed: "He'd done his looking. Why would he, when he came home to this?" Reed has never wondered how any of it works. Chapter One only touches this; Chapter Two explains how it works.

Cats, work and rewards

People choose Cats in areas they know or love: football, anime, films, music, medicine, law, accounting, and so on, usually for about eight hours a day. Roughly 80% of the population does this. A person still called a doctor may spend the day selecting between two medical Cats; an anime fan selects between anime girls. Everyone has a job: even choosing between two snowflakes contributes. Prestige no longer guarantees greater contribution. Stewards do more specialised work and are the ones who actually train the transistors (see Stewards below). The transistors may sometimes offer a slightly inferior alternative to learn something useful, but never so poor that people abandon them.

The reward system is adaptive, not punitive. Better contributions yield better and more personalised rewards and access. Lower-value selections adjust the timing or quality of rewards, never retaliation. Personalised offers come from impersonal interfaces and recommendations, never from a person or a personified transistor.

Abundance and money

The collapse in scarcity of digital expertise, plus transistor productivity, has created enormous abundance. Ordinary people all have their own good house, with a drive and a garden. Anyone can pick any car they like, from a long list, and change it whenever they please; cars drive themselves. Reed's is long, low and silver; no real brand names are ever used. There are no trams in the story; people go by car.

No money in 2038. Cat work began around 2027 as ordinary paid employment in normal money. Score-based rewards spread gradually over years as Cat work spread, until money faded away rather than being abolished. By 2038 no one pays for anything. Characters can remember paying money; they never describe present services as "cheap" or "free." Scarce desirable goods (a particular cut of beef, rare items, land) may still depend on contributions. Reed's beef disappointment is petty and comic, not deprivation.

Former professionals such as doctors, lawyers, actors and celebrities may resent losing prestige while living far better than before. Some refuse Cat work and may end up homeless as a result of refusal, not scarcity. Most people adapt and are delighted.

The Elite / Owners

The Elite own the transistors, which is where the power sits, and also the machines the transistors run on (called "the coil" in-world; keep it vague, never decodable as any real company or person). They do not own the world's electricity. They are not evil, cruel or scheming. They are doing what genuinely makes society prosper, and it mostly works. Their decisions, like everything to do with the transistors, are completely rational. The reader's unease comes from rationality followed all the way through, not from villainy.

Up until around 2028 everyone knew who they were; they were on the television all the time. Then, as Cat work spread, they disappeared from public view. People briefly wondered whether they had been murdered; they had not, and everyone knows that. They simply had no reason to be seen: everything on television is selected by people picking Cats, so there is no need for the Elite to appear. Their withdrawal is ordinary, not sinister. Everyone still knows exactly who they are: if one walked down a Brackton street, every person would recognise him instantly. They could go out; they simply don't need to. Never name or portray any real person. Their physical safety remains a non-negotiable constraint. The Elite are not hated by ordinary people.

Stewards are the people who train the transistors and are the only ordinary-ish people with real contact with the Elite. Brutes are in contact with the Elite only because they guard them. Do not add any other intermediary class. The Elite have not appeared in Chapter One; in-world people may call them "the Owners."

Stewards

"Stewards" is the in-world name for the people once called model trainers (that phrase is writer-only shorthand and never appears in the manuscript). The name is meant to sound a little glamorous, a cut above an ordinary trade, but not grand: they look after what the Elite own. Behind the scenes they do the whole job: gather everyone's choices from the Cats, train the transistors on them, update the rewards, and keep the transistor processes running across everything. So when the radio knows Reed's taste, no person is doing it by hand; the transistor is, and the Stewards keep it learning. A tiny subset of Stewards make the Mags, so few they're barely recognised as a separate group.

Stewards are not cruel or scheming; they are doing a job too. They have better privileges than Cat workers, including very large homes. Because they know how Mags work, they are not steered by them the way Cat workers are, and they use Mags for other purposes, not as companions or pleasure. Ordinary people regard them with vague, uncurious respect and never ask what they do.

Rough proportions (working figures, not a census): about 80% Cat workers, about 10% Brutes, fewer than 9% Stewards, and a very small Elite.

The Brutes: the central exception

Brutes are completely human. They are actual flesh-and-blood men, and the story states this plainly. They are not robots, Mags, cyborgs or machines.

Origin. Most Brutes are older now. They came from the men who, during the transition when everyone else was taking up Cat work, were the construction workers, soldiers and strongest physical men. Because the transistors can optimise the human body completely, they were genetically enhanced to an extreme, pumped full of growth hormones and the like, until they were built far beyond any natural man. (This supersedes earlier notes that Brutes were selected at birth and raised from youth.) The enhancements, injections and conditioning are passed on to later generations of Brutes, so the class continues. Brutes are about 10% of the population.

Appearance: towering, stubble, cropped hair, tired eyes; black kit plated at the shoulders and chest; a headset over one ear through which they receive instructions; BRUTE stencilled in white on the upper arm.

Work. They do what Mags cannot: heavy physical work, military and combat work, Elite protection, the kind of things too hard for Mags to manage. This reason is never spelled out in the text. They are not police of ordinary Cat workers and never intimidate Reed over his work.

Conditioning. Brutes are constantly brainwashed and manipulated to always obey the Elite, and to obey and never be unkind to ordinary people. If a Brute tries to hurt an ordinary person, another Brute must kill him; trying to disobey or turn on the Elite triggers the same. They can be compelled to do anything, even harm friends. They receive continual propaganda that they are stupid and inferior, are publicly ridiculed, and are segregated. Their depression and lack of freedom are real. They have it worse than anyone else in the story, by a long way.

Why, in one reason only: the Elite and Stewards are so few, and the Brutes are strong enough that, if they wanted to, they could beat the Elite and take everything, and the whole system would collapse. So their obedience must be absolute. This is rational, not cruel for its own sake, which is what makes it horrifying. Ordinary citizens ridicule them partly because that prevents sympathy. Show this through offhand remarks, never explain it. Do not write a Brute rebellion plot or imply one is possible. Reed is never a Brute; the Brutes' suffering is real but not representative of the happy majority.

Politics, news and shared reality

Transistors generate personalised news and political entertainment, coordinated so people's experiences don't collide. This remains separate from the transistors' factual reliability in medicine, law and ordinary services. Never explain with technical jargon.

Characters

• Winston Reed: see above. Never referred to as "Winston" alone. Use Reed, Mr. Reed, or Winston Reed.
• Grace: Reed's Mag partner, built to his own ideal, attentive and tuned to his preferences, quietly steering him toward what's good for him. Faint clean not-quite-anything smell (not soap). Over the years she has become better company than any person he has met.
• Former roommate (unnamed): in 2027 he took an anime Cat offer at the same time Reed took football. His job is picking between anime girls he likes, which pays off later in the story. He now has a house with a garden and a remarkable car. Reed has no current roommate.
• Charleston: selects advertising/visual Cats (lettering, image arrangement). Cheerful, never ill-spirited.
• Benjamin: selects music Cats. Cheerful, never ill-spirited; a fair man.
• Scott: Reed and the lads' old mate. Twenty years on heroin ("the skag"), through more than fifty rehabs over those years, none of which took. The transistor got him off it in one morning; he doesn't even think about it now and has a Cat job. The others he used with got off it just as easily. Mentioned by Benjamin in Chapter One as resounding proof of how well the world works, and why the doctor's gloom is only pride. Told cheerfully and offhand, never as a case study.
• The doctor at the Crown: unnamed; resents losing his prestige while living very well. Do not name him until the author chooses.
• Jeffrey: unresolved; do not assign a role without author approval.
• Former celebrities: film stars, singers and rappers from around 2026, whose work became automated. They refused Cat work, protested, were told they weren't needed for that anymore, and some ended up homeless by refusal. Press photographers still take their pictures; they don't look happy. A down-on-his-luck former celebrity meeting Reed and the lads is a promising comic scene.
• At the Crown, the lads call Reed "Captain Football" when they're pleased with him.

Chapter structure

Chapter One establishes the world well but not completely. It ends with Reed coming home to Grace and a light, unexplained touch of how Mags learn their person to keep them satisfied. Chapter Two goes into how that works.

Open questions — deliberately not forced

1. Food production, physical scarcity and access to rare goods.
2. The precise economic mechanisms of lasting hierarchy amid abundance.
3. The exact mechanics of how Brute enhancements and conditioning carry into later generations.
4. Jeffrey, the friends' fuller roles, the sustained plot and ending.
5. How occasional imperfect Cat outputs fit with near-universal accuracy.
6. How personalised politics coexists with factual reliability elsewhere.

Chapter Two continuity (15 October 2026, author-authorised)

Chapter Two takes place on the Thursday and Friday after Chapter One (Chapter One's day is a Wednesday). The working file is chapter22038.md.
• Reed usually does his Cats at home in the back room, but on Thursdays he goes into the old Corn Exchange on Bridge Street ("the Exchange"), fitted out with long tables, for the company of other football Cat workers.
• Hollin Rise: the part of Brackton where the Stewards live, up past the ridge road and the reservoir. Large, mostly glass houses set far back behind long lawns and low stone walls. Reed's car takes the ridge road because he once said he liked the view.
• A Steward's Mag is plain and grey with no real face; the Steward opens a panel and looks inside it "the way a man peers under a bonnet." Reed is puzzled why anyone would choose one like that ("Takes all sorts"). The reason is never explained.

• The reward beat: Reed picks a funny dive that the whole Crown hates; that evening no beef or pork is available to him, only chicken. Grace steers him gently ("It's a room you're picking for, though, isn't it, love"); next day he picks the crowd-pleasing options, is "Captain Football" again, and the beef returns. He is never really upset.
• How Mags learn: everything Reed does around Grace goes into her "the way his picks went into the football"; she knows the difference between what he wants and what keeps him right, and keeps him on the near side of it.
• Dominic Vale: a fictional film star from 2026, once on the side of every bus, now sleeping rough on a bench by the canal, photographed by the press. An old clip shows him protesting with other famous faces ("I am an artist. I am not sitting there all day choosing between —"); the clip always cuts off there. He was offered film Cats and refused.
• The lads call the Elite "the Owners." They remember them on the telly every night around 2027–2028; people's mams thought they'd been done in; they hadn't, they just stopped coming on.
• Brutes are seen carrying a steel beam to a new building by the canal. Brutes are now noticed "the way you noticed a car going past" (not a lorry or freight train).
• Grace leaves Reed a bacon roll in the car door pocket.

Writing rule

Resolve open questions through story, never as a technical explainer. It is a comedy first. Never make the reader feel dumb or over-explained; keep it warm and immediately relatable, the way the Chapter One Grace scene feels like a slightly-further-along version of everyday life. Any chill is gentle, a matter-of-fact "that's just how it is," never sinister, never preachy, never forced. Above all, Winston Reed remains a funny, happy lad living an extraordinarily good ordinary life.
